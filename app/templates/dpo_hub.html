What is a Data Protection Officer?

A DPO is the person inside an organisation whose entire job is to protect people's personal data. They are like a security guard, but instead of guarding a building, they guard information.

Every organisation that handles large amounts of personal data must have one under GDPR Article 37. The DPO makes sure the organisation follows data protection laws, responds to breaches properly, and respects people's rights over their own data.

The DPO can answer one question at any time: "Is our organisation handling people's data properly and legally?"

The 7 Responsibilities
1 Know Your Data (ROPA)

GDPR Article 30

Before you can protect data you need to know what you have. The DPO documents what personal data the organisation collects, why it is collected, who can access it, and how long it is kept.

Without this: You cannot protect what you do not know exists. If the ICO asks what data you process and you cannot answer, you are already breaking the law.

2 Check Compliance

GDPR Article 5

The DPO regularly checks whether the organisation is following GDPR principles: only collecting data that is needed, keeping it accurate, deleting it when no longer required, and storing it securely.

Without this: Problems build up silently until the ICO audits you and finds violations that could have been caught and fixed months ago.

3 Assess Risks (DPIA)

GDPR Article 35

Before the organisation starts any new project involving personal data, the DPO assesses the risks. What data is involved? What could go wrong? How likely is it? How bad would it be? What can we do to prevent it?

Without this: You launch a new system, it has a flaw, data gets exposed, and you face a breach that could have been prevented with a 30-minute risk assessment.

4 Handle Requests (SAR)

GDPR Articles 15-22

People have the right to ask what data you hold about them, ask for corrections, ask for deletion, or get a portable copy. When they do, the DPO must respond within 30 days.

Without this: Ignoring a data request is illegal. The person can complain to the ICO and the organisation faces enforcement action.

5 Detect Breaches

GDPR Articles 33-34

When personal data is accessed, lost, or leaked without authorisation, that is a breach. The DPO must detect it, assess the severity, report it to the ICO within 72 hours if serious, and notify affected individuals if the risk is high.

Without this: A breach goes unnoticed for weeks. By the time someone finds out, the damage is done and you have missed the 72-hour reporting deadline. Fines can reach €20 million.

6 Review Policies

GDPR Article 24

Every organisation needs written policies on how they handle personal data — privacy policy, data retention policy, access control policy, breach response policy. The DPO makes sure these exist, cover everything, and stay up to date.

Without this: The ICO asks to see your data protection policies. You either do not have them or they are three years old and reference laws that no longer exist. That is a compliance failure.

7 Train Staff

GDPR Article 39(1)(a)

Most data breaches happen because of human mistakes — clicking phishing links, emailing data to the wrong person, leaving screens unlocked. The DPO trains everyone in the organisation on how to handle personal data safely.

Without this: An untrained employee emails a spreadsheet of customer details to the wrong address. That is a breach, and it happened because nobody told them not to.

Key GDPR Articles
Article 5 — Data Protection Principles

The foundation of GDPR. Data must be collected lawfully, for a specific purpose, kept accurate, not stored longer than needed, and kept secure. The accountability principle means you must be able to prove you follow these rules.

Article 33 — Breach Notification (72 hours)

If a breach happens, report it to the ICO within 72 hours. The report must say what happened, what data was affected, how many people, what the likely consequences are, and what you are doing to fix it.

Articles 37-39 — The DPO Role

Article 37 says when a DPO is needed. Article 38 says they must be given proper resources and independence. Article 39 defines their job: monitor compliance, advise on data protection, oversee impact assessments, and be the contact point for regulators.

Quick Glossary
DPO

Data Protection Officer — the person responsible for data protection in an organisation

GDPR

General Data Protection Regulation — the EU/UK law governing personal data

ICO

Information Commissioner's Office — the UK's data protection regulator

ROPA

Records of Processing Activities — a document listing all data an organisation processes

DPIA

Data Protection Impact Assessment — a risk check done before new data processing

SAR

Subject Access Request — when someone asks what data you hold about them

Personal Data

Any information that can identify a person — name, email, phone, IP address, photos

Data Breach

When personal data is accessed, lost, or shared without permission

Lawful Basis

The legal reason for collecting someone's data — consent, contract, legal obligation etc

Data Controller

The organisation that decides what data to collect and how to use it
